1 Introduction and Background

In contemporary society, digital communication platforms such as electronic mail, short message services (SMS), instant messaging applications, and social networks have become essential for academic, professional, and personal activities. Along with this rapid digital transformation, everyday internet users are exposed to an increasing volume of deceptive digital interactions. Attackers continuously deploy misleading messages, impersonated bank notifications, artificial security alerts, and deceptive links designed to manipulate human trust.

While cybersecurity professionals have access to sophisticated defensive tooling, Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) systems, threat intelligence feeds, and automated sandbox environments, ordinary internet users possess few tools suited to their needs. When non-technical individuals encounter a suspicious message, their available options are typically limited to searching online search engines, consulting acquaintances, or guessing whether a link is benign.

Standard security software often generates cryptic warning codes, technical jargon (such as domain mismatch, reverse DNS lookup failure, or certificate authority error), or unhelpful binary flags without explaining the underlying threat mechanism. This technical barrier leaves non-technical users vulnerable to manipulation.

The motivation behind the CyberSafe project is to bridge this gap between technical threat detection and practical user comprehension. The project proposes a unified, accessible cybersecurity platform where any user can submit suspicious digital content and receive an immediate, grounded assessment explaining what the threat is, why it is dangerous, and what concrete steps must be taken to remain protected.

2 Problem Statement

Everyday internet users frequently encounter suspicious digital communication but lack the technical background to evaluate risks effectively. Specifically, non-technical users face four interrelated challenges:

  • Identification Difficulty: Users cannot easily distinguish between authentic communications and sophisticated deceptive lures, including typosquatted domains, shortened redirection links, spoofed sender headers, and manipulative social engineering cues.
  • Threat Comprehension Barrier: Existing security tools produce technical reports suited for security analysts rather than ordinary citizens. Users are informed that a link is "flagged" or "untrusted," but are not given understandable context explaining the operational mechanics of the attack.
  • Impact Ambiguity: When faced with an alert, users often fail to understand what assets are genuinely at risk, such as session credentials, one-time passwords (OTPs), financial data, device integrity, or personal privacy.
  • Decision Paralysis and Action Vacuum: In the absence of step-by-step guidance, users often panic, ignore valid warnings, or take counter-productive actions (such as replying to a scammer or clicking links to "cancel" a fake subscription).

Therefore, there is an urgent need for a unified platform that intakes untrusted inputs, performs multi-layered deterministic and statistical security analysis, and translates technical findings into clear, calibrated, and actionable guidance for people of all technical backgrounds.

3 Project Objectives

The proposed CyberSafe project will pursue the following specific objectives:

  1. Develop a Unified Multi-Modal Intake Pipeline: To enable non-technical users to submit suspicious content through three everyday modalities: screenshots of interfaces, raw message text, and direct website URLs.
  2. Implement Multi-Layered Threat Detection: To combine deterministic rule-based pattern matching, statistical machine learning feature classification, and network/domain security checks to identify threats without relying solely on generative artificial intelligence.
  3. Formulate an Evidence-Grounded Risk Scoring Engine: To synthesize findings from parallel analysis engines into a mathematical risk score with clear severity tiers (Low, Medium, High) and protective ambiguity fail-safe boundaries.
  4. Design a Personalized Multi-Tier Explanation Architecture: To translate raw technical telemetry into three user-selectable explanation tiers (Simple, Detailed, and Technical), ensuring that the same verified findings can be understood by users with varying security literacy.
  5. Synthesize Actionable Remediation Plans: To automatically produce structured, step-by-step user instructions detailing what happened, why the content is risky, and what prioritized actions should be executed.
  6. Enforce Defensive Security Engineering: To safeguard the platform against Server-Side Request Forgery (SSRF) and automatically redact Personally Identifiable Information (PII) before external processing.
  7. Promote Digital Security Literacy: To educate users on foundational cybersecurity concepts (such as the CIA Triad, multi-factor authentication, and social engineering patterns) during routine threat assessments.
  8. Establish an Extensible Architecture: To structure the platform modularly so that future capabilities (such as browser extensions, network monitoring, and enterprise dashboards) can be integrated without redesigning core pipeline stages.

4 Scope of the Project

To maintain academic rigor and project feasibility, the scope of CyberSafe is explicitly divided into the proposed core implementation scope for this course project and an extended scope reserved for future development.

A. Initial / Core Proposed Scope

Target deliverables for the academic foundation project:

  • Multi-Modal Intake: Direct ingestion of screenshots, pasted text, and website URLs.
  • Optical Character Recognition (OCR): Image pre-processing and text extraction from uploaded screenshots.
  • Text & Header Parsing: Extraction of email headers, embedded hyperlinks, telephone numbers, and urgency phrases.
  • URL Parsing & Sanitization: Domain normalization, punycode decoding, and redirect unpacking.
  • Defensive SSRF Shield: Socket-level IP validation blocking loopback (127.0.0.0/8), private subnets (RFC 1918), and cloud metadata IPs.
  • Client-Side PII Masking: Automatic redaction of credit cards, passwords, and sensitive identifiers.
  • Parallel Security Analysis: Deterministic signature rules, ML-based lexical/entropy classifiers, and domain/SSL checks.
  • Eight Core Threat Categories: Phishing, malicious URLs, social engineering, credential theft, basic malware indicators, account alerts, web vulnerabilities, and network indicators.
  • Multi-Tiered Explanation: User-configurable Simple, Detailed, and Technical output tiers.
  • Action Plan Generator: Interactive three-part remediation checklist for end users.

B. Future / Extended Scope

Potential capabilities for subsequent project phases:

  • Real-Time Browser Extension: Automated inspection of links and active DOM elements during browsing.
  • Native Mobile Application: Direct operating system share-sheet integration for SMS and chat apps.
  • Mail Server Integration: Real-time API connectors for Google Workspace and Microsoft 365 inboxes.
  • Dynamic Sandbox Execution: Virtualized detonation chamber for safe runtime analysis of executable files.
  • Intrusion Detection System (IDS) Telemetry: Ingestion of packet capture (PCAP) logs and firewall alert streams.
  • Enterprise SIEM Dashboard: Centralized team threat tracking, incident triage, and aggregate reporting.
  • Automated Threat Blacklist Sync: Bidirectional integration with public threat intelligence feeds (STIX/TAXII).
  • Live Account Breach Monitoring: Integration with compromised credential databases for automated exposure alerts.

5 Proposed Solution & Technical Architecture

CyberSafe proposes a clean, five-stage asynchronous pipeline architecture. The fundamental engineering principle of the system is the strict separation of concerns between technical threat detection and natural language explanation.

5.1 Stage-by-Stage Operational Workflow

The execution flow proceeds sequentially across five distinct stages to guarantee both mathematical accuracy and plain-language accessibility:

Stage 1: Input Processor & Pre-processing Shield

When a user provides input via the web or mobile interface, the request is directed to the Input Processor. For screenshots, an Optical Character Recognition (OCR) pre-parser extracts textual tokens, measures text entropy, and flags blurry or unreadable submissions. For raw text, email headers, embedded links, and telephone numbers are extracted. For URLs, punycode strings are normalized and redirect hops are evaluated. Simultaneously, a defensive Server-Side Request Forgery (SSRF) shield inspects destination IP resolutions before socket creation to block loopback interfaces (127.0.0.1) and internal network blocks (10.0.0.0/8, 192.168.0.0/16, and cloud metadata 169.254.169.254). Personal identifiers such as passwords, payment cards, and phone numbers are scrubbed to protect user privacy.

Stage 2: Parallel Security Analysis Engines

The normalized content is dispatched simultaneously to three independent security engines running asynchronously:

  • Deterministic Rules Engine: Executes calibrated regex heuristics and signature checks. It identifies social-engineering urgency phrases (such as "immediate action required" or "account will be suspended within 24 hours"), credential solicitation traps, brand typosquatting ("Paypa1", "Micros0ft"), and known link-shortener domains.
  • Machine Learning Feature Classifier: Evaluates lightweight lexical and entropy vectors, including URL character entropy, count of subdomains, abnormal token frequencies, and domain age statistics to emit a statistical threat probability.
  • Security Checks Engine: Conducts deterministic infrastructure verifications including SSL/TLS certificate validity, certificate lifetime remaining, sender SPF/DKIM/DMARC alignment (for email headers), and domain age verification via WHOIS queries.

Stage 3: Risk Synthesis & Scoring Formulation

The Risk Engine synthesizes the telemetry from Stage 2 into a single unified mathematical verdict. It calculates a weighted composite score using predefined, calibratable coefficients:

Proposed Scoring Formula:
risk_score = (0.35 * rules_score) + (0.35 * ml_probability) + (0.30 * failed_checks_ratio)
Where rules_score is normalized between 0.0 and 1.0, ml_probability denotes the classifier's threat estimation, and failed_checks_ratio represents the proportion of failed technical validations.

The resulting numerical score maps into three calibrated severity tiers:

  • High Risk (Score >= 0.75): Strong presence of malicious indicators, urgent manipulation, or known blacklisted infrastructure.
  • Medium Risk (0.40 <= Score < 0.75): Ambiguous indicators, suspicious redirection chains, or newly registered domains lacking verified reputations.
  • Low Risk (Score < 0.40): Benign structural patterns, valid cryptographic certificates, and absence of deceptive keywords.

Fail-Safe Ambiguity Boundary: A vital defensive principle of the platform is the fail-safe boundary rule. If a computed score falls within a narrow boundary margin (+/- 0.03 of a threshold, such as 0.73 or 0.38), the system will automatically promote the verdict to the higher risk category. In cybersecurity, the cost of a false negative (failing to alert a user to an active scam) is catastrophic, whereas the cost of a conservative warning is negligible.

Stage 4: LLM Explanation Layer (Crucial Design Principle)

Fundamental AI Design Principle: The proposed platform does not rely on a Large Language Model as an unconstrained threat detector. Generative AI models are susceptible to hallucination, prompt injection, and inconsistent decision boundaries. Therefore, the LLM will serve exclusively as an explanation and communication layer. It will be provided with strictly grounded telemetry from the Risk Engine (verified rule triggers, domain age, certificate state, and threat category) and tasked with translating those findings into clear language.

To serve users of all backgrounds, the explanation engine will produce three simultaneous explanation tiers:

Tier 1: Simple

Audience: Non-technical users, school students, elderly individuals.

"This message looks like a scam. Do not click the link or share your password or OTP with anyone."

Tier 2: Detailed

Audience: General professionals, small business staff, undergraduate students.

"This message contains several phishing indicators, including urgent language, a suspicious link, and an unauthorized request for confidential login details."

Tier 3: Technical

Audience: IT staff, system administrators, security students.

"Potential credential harvesting attack. Indicators include lookalike domain structure, absence of SPF/DKIM validation, and social engineering urgency cues."

Resilient Deterministic Fallback: In the event of external API throttling, network timeouts, or latency exceeding 3.0 seconds, the platform will immediately bypass the LLM and employ an internal deterministic template synthesizer. This guarantees that the user receives an immediate, accurate security assessment even if cloud AI services are degraded.

Stage 5: Action Plan Generator

The final stage organizes the assessment into three structured, intuitive sections:

  1. What Happened: A plain-language summary of the observed digital event (e.g., "You received an SMS claiming your bank card has been blocked due to suspicious activity").
  2. Why It Is Risky: A transparent explanation of the real-world hazard (e.g., "The link leads to an unverified website designed to steal your debit card details and OTP").
  3. What To Do: A prioritized, numbered checklist guiding the user to safety (e.g., 1. Do not click the link; 2. Never disclose one-time passwords; 3. Contact the bank using the phone number printed on the physical card).

7 Project Proposal Summary

The CyberSafe project represents a comprehensive, student-led proposal to democratize cybersecurity threat detection. By combining multi-modal ingestion (screenshots, text, and URLs), parallel deterministic and machine-learning security analysis, grounded risk scoring, and a multi-tiered natural language explanation layer, the platform will empower non-technical internet users to make informed, safe decisions.

This Unit Test 2 proposal outlines a rigorous, modular design that strictly adheres to sound software and security principles: the LLM is restricted to a grounded communication role, system boundaries are defended against SSRF and data leakage, and verified telemetry is translated into actionable guidance. Upon approval, the project team will proceed with the implementation of the core proposed pipeline for the academic term.
